    How to record so the measurement is worth keeping
    • Within 5 to 10 cm. Published work found phones pick up a mosquito at 50 mm for a basic handset and 100 mm for a better one. Beyond that there is no signal to measure, however good the software is.
    • Quiet, below about 50 dB. A fan, a road or a television will bury the tone.
    • Turn off noise suppression. This app already asks the browser to disable echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain, because all three are designed to remove exactly this kind of faint steady tone.
    • Record the true species. A recording without a confirmed identification cannot train anything. Catch, record, then identify under a microscope, and type the species in before saving.
    • Note the temperature. Wingbeat frequency changes with it, so a recording without a temperature is worth much less.
    What this instrument can and cannot tell you

    It measures a frequency honestly. The self test confirms it recovers a known tone to within about 1 Hz, including when the fundamental is quieter than its own harmonics.

    It now also runs two trained neural networks, described in the next section. One answers whether a mosquito is flying near the microphone. The other asks whether that mosquito is Culex quinquefasciatus. Neither was trained on a single Indian recording.

    It does not identify species in general, and nothing on a phone reliably does yet. In the published field trial, classification from phone audio reached roughly 35% accuracy without a location prior and about 65% with one. Some pairs are hopeless: Aedes aegypti was taken for Anopheles gambiae about 95% of the time. Others separate well, including Ae. aegypti from Ae. albopictus, and Culex pipiens from Cx. quinquefasciatus.

    So the value is in counting, not naming. Relative abundance in one band, at one site, tracked over weeks, is a usable surveillance signal even when any single recording is uncertain.

    And the real output of this app is the dataset. A model for Tamil Nadu vectors needs Tamil Nadu recordings, with confirmed species and temperature. That is what the export is for.

    What the two trained models are, and what they are not

    Where they come from. Both were trained on HumBugDB, the largest public mosquito acoustic dataset: 9,295 files, 33.5 hours, recorded in Tanzania, Thailand, the United Kingdom, Kenya and the USA. There are no Indian recordings in it at all.

    The detector answers one question: is a mosquito flying near this microphone? That question has real data behind it, 18.3 hours of mosquito against 14.6 hours of background, and the answer is species independent, so it travels better than any species model would.

    The Culex quinquefasciatus head had 678 files and 124 minutes to learn from, which is the most any single species in the dataset offers. Cx. quinquefasciatus is a genuine Tamil Nadu vector, carrying lymphatic filariasis and Japanese encephalitis, so it is the right species to start with. But wingbeat frequency is set largely by body size, and body size differs between populations. Treat its answer as a hypothesis your own recordings will correct.

    Why the other three vectors are missing. The public data holds 22 minutes of Aedes aegypti, 72 seconds of Anopheles stephensi and 42 seconds of Aedes albopictus. Forty-two seconds cannot train anything. That gap is now measured rather than assumed, and it is the clearest argument there is for collecting Indian recordings, which is what the export button on this page is for.

    What runs on the phone. 23,553 parameters, 92 KB per model, in plain JavaScript with no machine-learning library. The JavaScript was checked against the original Python model and agrees to better than one part in ten million, and the audio features were checked against librosa to better than a thousandth of a decibel. Those checks matter more than they sound: a model fed slightly different numbers than it was trained on does not fail loudly, it returns confident nonsense.
